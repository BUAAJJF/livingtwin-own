Plan

The object area is where things are put for the robot to tidy; the camera cone is its 66° horizontal field of view, clipped to the table. The bin sits 36° to the right, outside the object area, so nothing spawns on top of it.

Elevation

Section taken through the camera and the base. The camera looks at a point 700 mm away, 30 mm above the table, roughly the middle of the object area.

What it should see

Six frames from the simulated camera at this pose: near is bright, far is dark, and the target object is tinted. Use these to check the real mounting — the arm should sit in the upper middle, the bin should be fully inside the left edge with a margin, and the near table should fill the bottom third. If the bin is clipped, the camera is aimed too far left.

Resolution≥ 224 × 168 What the policy consumes. A higher-resolution sensor is fine and gets downsampled; the number that matters is the field of view.

Building it

  1. Set the robot on the table and treat the centre of its base, at the table surface, as the origin. +x is the direction the base faces, +y is to its left.
  2. Mark the object area and the bin position on the table first — they are what the camera has to frame, and they are easier to measure than a camera on a stand.
  3. Mount the camera so its lens centre lands on the plan position. A frame or a clamp arm is easier to hold steady than a tripod at this height, and the arm never comes closer than 271 mm to it even flailing.
  4. Aim it at a marker placed at the aim point (321, 71) on the table. Do not aim it by eye at the object area — the aim point is deliberately biased towards the bin.
  5. Compare a live depth frame against the six above. This catches a mis-aimed camera in seconds, long before calibration would.
  6. Calibrate properly: mount a board on the gripper, drive 20+ poses spanning the object area, solve eye-to-hand for camera → base.

Two things worth knowing before you mount anything

Precision is not the goal. The policy is trained with the camera pose randomised around this nominal — roughly ±20 mm and ±2° — so calibration only has to land inside that envelope, not hit the number. That is also why the field of view is 52° rather than the 45° that would just barely fit: the extra margin is what a mis-mounted camera spends. Mount it solidly, though; a camera that moves after calibration is a camera that is wrong.

The bin side is the one place it must not go. Of eight candidate viewpoints, the one placed near the bin was the only failure: the target object was visible in 58% of frames against 100% everywhere else, because the bin walls occlude the object area and the view runs nearly along the table. It is the position that feels right — close to where the placing happens — and it is the one that does not work.
